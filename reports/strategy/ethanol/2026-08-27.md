# Ethanol Strategy Brief
## Week of August 27, 2026

## Executive View

- **Field evidence materially challenged USDA’s corn balance sheet.** Pro Farmer estimated a **173.2-bushel national yield and 15.344-billion-bushel crop**, versus USDA’s controlling August estimates of **180.7 bushels and 16.013 billion bushels**. Nearby corn closed at **$5.103 per bushel, up $0.315, or 6.6%, for the week**, confirming that the market increased—not reduced—the yield-risk premium.
- **Corn procurement is now the dominant plant-margin risk.** The 669-million-bushel gap between Pro Farmer and USDA would, if mechanically applied before demand rationing, reduce USDA’s **1.653-billion-bushel projected carryout to roughly 984 million bushels**. That is an illustrative sensitivity, not a forecast, but it shows why delayed harvest and first-quarter coverage became more exposed.
- **RFS credit risk deteriorated before any final EPA action.** D6 RINs reportedly fell **34 cents to $1.75 on August 24** after EPA signaled an extension of the September 1 compliance deadline and an end-August decision on pending small-refinery exemptions. Administration officials are reportedly considering relief covering **1.2–1.8 billion RINs**, but EPA said no decisions had been made.
- **The ethanol balance improved only at the margin.** Production rebounded **23,000 barrels per day to 1.112 million**, while inventories rose another **85,000 barrels to 25.206 million**, 11.8% above last year. Stronger reported exports helped slow the stock build, but output remains high relative to domestic inputs.
- **Energy provided a stronger ethanol price umbrella but higher plant costs.** RBOB rose **3.7%** and gasoline stocks fell 2.536 million barrels, while Henry Hub rose **6.4%**. Midwest gas storage remains 5.9% above its five-year average, limiting scarcity risk but not preventing a near-term cost increase.
- **Overall margin and risk conditions deteriorated:** sharply higher corn and natural-gas futures, combined with weaker RIN values, outweighed firmer gasoline and the slower ethanol inventory build.

## 1. Crop tour reversed the prior week’s corn-risk improvement

**Status:** REFUTE

**What happened**

Pro Farmer’s August 21 national estimate placed corn yield at **173.2 bushels per acre**, with a range of 171.5–175.0, and production at **15.344 billion bushels**, plus or minus 1%. Scouts reported lower ear populations, shorter grain length and substantial field-to-field variability. USDA’s August 12 survey-based forecast remains 180.7 bushels and 16.013 billion bushels. (Pro Farmer, *“Pro Farmer Estimates 2026 U.S. Corn Crop at 15.344 Billion Bushels,”* August 21, 2026.) ([profarmer.com](https://www.profarmer.com/news/pro-farmer-estimates-2026-u-s-corn-crop-15-344-billion-bushels-soybeans-4-572-billion-bushels))

USDA subsequently cut national corn condition by three points to **57% good-to-excellent** for the week ended August 23, versus 60% the prior week and 71% last year. The crop was 81% in dough, 41% dented and 6% mature. (USDA NASS, *Crop Progress*, August 24, 2026.) ([nass.usda.gov](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Publications/Todays_Reports/reports/prog3426.pdf))

**Why it matters**

The new field evidence does not prove that USDA is seven-plus bushels too high; crop-tour estimates are not equivalent to USDA’s survey process. However, the magnitude and geographic consistency of the disappointment are too large to dismiss as routine sampling noise. Nearby corn above $5 and up 31.5 cents validates the conclusion that the trade now assigns materially greater probability to tighter supply.

**Risk-manager implication**

Plants that treated recent rainfall as justification for leaving harvest or winter corn uncovered now face greater futures risk. The first transmission is through December and March corn; local basis will depend on whether tour weakness overlaps each plant’s procurement radius. Directionally: **corn bullish, ethanol feedstock cost higher and physical margins lower**, with high confidence through the September crop reports.

**Watch next**

Whether early harvest results validate low ear counts and whether USDA moves yield closer to the Pro Farmer range.

## 2. Prospective refinery exemptions weakened RIN protection

**Status:** UPDATE

**What happened**

EPA plans to extend the current **September 1, 2026** deadline for refiners to demonstrate 2025 RFS compliance; a final rule entered White House regulatory review on August 26. EPA also indicated it expected to decide pending small-refinery petitions by the end of August. (Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs, *“RFS Program: Extension of 2025 Compliance Reporting Deadline,”* received August 26, 2026.) ([reginfo.gov](https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/eoDetails?rrid=1512912&utm_source=openai))

Reuters reported that D6 RINs fell to **$1.75 on August 24**, their lowest since April 15. Subsequent reporting said administration officials were pressing for exemptions covering approximately **1.2–1.8 billion RINs**, versus roughly 1 billion previously contemplated. EPA stated that no petition decisions had been made. (Reuters, *“US Ethanol Credit Prices Slump After EPA Delays Biofuel Compliance Deadline,”* August 24; *“White House Pushes for More Refinery Waivers,”* August 26, 2026.) ([investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/commodities-news/us-ethanol-credit-prices-slump-after-epa-delays-biofuel-compliance-deadline-4874064?utm_source=openai))

**Why it matters**

The immediate impact is on RIN value and obligated-party compliance strategy, not necessarily physical ethanol disappearance. Still, exemptions materially above the assumptions embedded in EPA’s 2026 RVO would weaken the marginal incentive to blend and reduce the value of RIN-linked ethanol contracts.

**Risk-manager implication**

Treat the reported exemption range as a scenario, not confirmed policy. Plants should separate outright ethanol exposure from D6-linked pricing and avoid assuming the record headline RVO fully protects credit value. The largest near-term exposure is **RIN price and counterparty contract value**, followed by physical blend demand if EPA does not offset exemptions.

## 3. EPA resolved the late-August E15 cliff

**Status:** RESOLVE

**What happened**

EPA’s August 20 waiver extended the common national gasoline pool containing **9%–15% ethanol through August 31**, closing the previously perceived August 29–31 gap. Beginning September 1, EPA permits the earlier transition to higher-volatility winter-grade gasoline through the remainder of the federal summer-control period. (EPA, *“National Fuel Waiver to Bring Forward Wintertime Gasoline Beginning September 1, 2026,”* August 20, 2026.) ([epa.gov](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2026-08/national-fuel-waiver-governors-8-20-2026.pdf))

**Why it matters**

This resolves the immediate E15 distribution concern identified last week. Once winter-grade gasoline becomes permissible, the summer RVP restriction no longer creates the same E15 constraint. The trade-off is that earlier winter-grade supply can pressure petroleum blending-component values.

**Risk-manager implication**

The result is **supportive for continued E15 availability but not a new structural demand mandate**. With RBOB nevertheless rising 3.7% this week, the petroleum price umbrella strengthened despite the additional supply flexibility.

## 4. Exports slowed—but did not reverse—the ethanol stock build

**Status:** UPDATE

**What happened**

| EIA ethanol balance | Week ended Aug. 14 | Week ended Aug. 21 | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Production, million bpd | 1.089 | 1.112 | +0.023 |
| Inventories, million bbl | 25.121 | 25.206 | +0.085 |
| Refiner/blender inputs, million bpd | 0.926 | 0.923 | -0.003 |

Inventories remained **11.8% above the comparable 2025 week**. The Midwest accounted for most of the weekly build, rising 299,000 barrels to 10.045 million, while East and Gulf Coast stocks declined. (EIA, *Weekly Petroleum Status Report*, August 26, 2026.) ([eia.gov](https://www.eia.gov/dnav/pet/pet_stoc_wstk_a_epooxe_sae_mbbl_w.htm?utm_source=openai))

Gasoline inventories fell **2.536 million barrels to 206.842 million**, with refinery utilization at 97.4%, reinforcing the stronger RBOB environment. ([ir.eia.gov](https://ir.eia.gov/wpsr/table5.csv))

**Why it matters**

The stock increase was much smaller than the prior week’s 323,000-barrel build despite higher production, indicating better total disappearance. It does not yet resolve the underlying imbalance: inventories have increased for three consecutive reported weeks and remain historically heavy.

**Risk-manager implication**

Prompt ethanol exposure improved from “steadily worsening” to **heavy but stabilizing**. Plants still need export support or maintenance-season production cuts to produce an outright draw. Corn demand remains supported by the 1.112-million-barrel-per-day run rate, intensifying the feedstock squeeze created by the crop-tour estimate.

## Strategic Synthesis

The week created a more adverse plant-margin combination: **field evidence challenged corn supply just as plants maintained high operating rates, while prospective refinery exemptions reduced regulatory-credit protection**. Strong gasoline values and improved ethanol disappearance are meaningful offsets, but neither compensates for a 31.5-cent corn rally unless ethanol prices and basis respond further.
