Where we are

The 2025/26 corn marketing year ends August 31. Harvest bids and December futures are now the center of the new-crop decision.

USDA’s controlling August balance sheet puts the crop at 16.013 billion bushels, with a 180.7-bushel national yield and 1.653 billion bushels of 2026/27 ending stocks. Nebraska is forecast at 183 bushels per acre and 1.768 billion bushels. USDA cut national yield 2.3 bushels from July, but higher harvested acreage kept production essentially unchanged. Stronger exports cut projected carryout by 137 million bushels. (USDA, WASDE-674 and August Crop Production, August 12, 2026.) (s3.amazonaws.com)

Field evidence now challenges that yield. Pro Farmer estimated 173.2 bushels per acre and a 15.344-billion-bushel crop, 669 million bushels below USDA’s production forecast. (Pro Farmer, 2026 U.S. Corn Crop Estimate, August 21, 2026.) (profarmer.com)

Nearby corn was $5.103 per bushel, up 31.5 cents, or 6.6%, from August 20. December new-crop was $5.335, up 30 cents from $5.035. December carried roughly 23¼ cents over nearby, about 1½ cents less carry than last week. (Supplied market tape; Pro Farmer, August 20, and Barchart market data, August 27.) (profarmer.com)

The week in one page

  • Crop Tour materially challenged USDA. Pro Farmer’s 173.2-bushel national estimate is 7.5 bushels below USDA. Its Nebraska samples averaged 163.61 bushels, down 8.9% from last year’s tour and 5.6% below the three-year tour average. That does not prove USDA is wrong, but it puts the September yield revision squarely in play. (profarmer.com)
  • The board paid you. Nearby gained 31.5 cents and December gained 30 cents. That is a pricing opportunity for unpriced bushels, not a reason to remain fully exposed.
  • Weekly ratings supported the tour’s direction. U.S. corn fell to 57% G2E, down 3 points and versus 71% last year. Nebraska fell to 55% G2E, down 4 points and versus 78% last year. (nass.usda.gov)
  • Ethanol plants kept consuming corn, but inventories limited the enthusiasm. Production rose to 1.112 million barrels per day while stocks edged up to 25.206 million barrels. That is supportive for plant bids, not enough by itself to chase a weak basis. (agriculture.com)
  • Exports remain the demand test. The August 20 report showed 816,097 metric tons of new-crop sales, but total new-crop commitments of 11.39 million metric tons were running about 30% behind last year. USDA’s higher 3.275-billion-bushel export forecast still needs confirmation. (syngenta.ca)

Verdict: This week made it easier to raise your average sale price. The board handed you roughly 30 cents. Get some sold.

1. Crop Tour cut enough bushels to threaten the USDA carryout

Status: NEW

What happened

Pro Farmer’s national estimate was 15.344 billion bushels at 173.2 bushels per acre, with a production range of 15.191 billion to 15.498 billion. Scouts cited lower ear populations, shorter grain length and inconsistent field performance. Nebraska’s tour yield of 163.61 was particularly weak relative to both last year’s tour and its three-year tour average. (profarmer.com)

Tour numbers should not be substituted directly for USDA state yields. Pro Farmer adjusts tour observations for maturity, historical error and areas outside the route before producing its national estimate. Still, a 669-million-bushel production gap is too large to dismiss.

Why it matters for a long-corn producer

If the final crop moves materially toward Pro Farmer, USDA’s 1.653-billion-bushel carryout cannot stand without rationing demand or revising other balance-sheet lines. The market recognized that possibility: nearby corn rallied 6.6%.

Nebraska’s problem is uneven. Irrigated fields can still finish well while stressed dryland fields pull down whole-farm production. Do not let a good pivot corner talk you into selling bushels the dryland ground may not produce.

What to do with it

New-crop: Price another increment into the rally, but recheck ears, kernel depth and expected harvested acres first. August is no time to hedge a crop you no longer expect to have.

Old-crop: The nearby board paid more than 30 cents. If your basis is reasonable, finish more cash sales. If basis is poor, set futures or use an HTA and leave basis open.

Watch next

USDA’s September 11 Crop Production report is the next survey print capable of resolving the disagreement.

2. The rally is worth more than the remaining board carry

Status: CONFIRM

What happened

December reached $5.335, 30 cents above August 20. Nearby reached $5.103, up 31.5 cents. The December-nearby spread narrowed to about 23¼ cents. Crop uncertainty led the move, while Black Sea shipping concerns also added risk premium during the week. (Successful Farming, 3 Big Things Today, August 27, 2026.) (agriculture.com)

Why it matters for a long-corn producer

You were paid more in one week by the rally than the board currently offers for carrying corn from nearby into December. That does not mean empty every bin. It means holding unpriced grain now requires a deliberate basis or post-harvest carry plan.

What to do with it

Old-crop: Reward nearby. Do not pay interest and shrink to hold old corn merely because crop headlines remain bullish.

New-crop: Use December for incremental HTAs, forward cash sales or short hedges. If harvest basis is ugly, set the board and wait on basis. If the ethanol plant or feedlot bid strengthened with futures, take the cash bid.

USDA AMS publishes daily Nebraska bids, but no single statewide basis represents the east, central irrigated corridor and western feed market. Compare your exact delivery point and month rather than a state average. (USDA AMS, Nebraska Daily Elevator Grain Bids, accessed August 27, 2026.) (mymarketnews.ams.usda.gov)

3. Crop ratings confirm stress, but maturity is closing the weather window

Status: CONFIRM

What happened

National corn was 57% G2E, down 3 points. Nebraska was 55% G2E, down 4. Nebraska corn was 84% dough, 46% dented and only 3% mature. Nebraska topsoil was 43% short or very short, while subsoil was 57% short or very short. (USDA NASS, Crop Progress, and UNL CropWatch, August 24, 2026.) (nass.usda.gov)

Why it matters for a long-corn producer

The ratings reinforce the tour rather than resolve it. Rain can still protect kernel weight in later fields, but it cannot restore missing ears. As dent advances, test weight and kernel depth matter more than a green windshield view.

What to do with it

Re-size expected production before adding sales. Separate irrigated from dryland yield expectations. The 30-cent rally argues for pricing more; uncertain production argues against pricing beyond bushels you can reasonably defend.

Watch next

Kernel depth, premature drydown, stalk quality and early harvest reports will matter more than another routine one-point condition change.

4. Ethanol demand helped the local bid, but stocks kept it from being a clean bull story

Status: UPDATE

What happened

Ethanol production for the week ended August 21 rose 23,000 barrels per day to 1.112 million. Midwest output rose to 1.062 million barrels per day. Stocks increased 85,000 barrels to 25.206 million, while refiner and blender inputs slipped from 926,000 to 923,000 barrels per day. (agriculture.com)

RBOB gasoline gained 3.7% this week, supportive to blending economics. Natural gas rose 6.4%, but there is no evidence yet that higher energy costs reduced plant corn demand.

Why it matters for a long-corn producer

High run time means plants need corn. The inventory build means they do not need to chase every bushel at any price. Expect plant basis to depend heavily on each facility’s coverage and harvest timing.

Reports on August 27 said the White House was urging additional small-refinery waivers, but EPA had not issued a final decision by the report cutoff. That was a policy risk, not a confirmed change in corn disappearance. (agriculture.com)

What to do with it

Shop ethanol bids against elevators and shuttle loaders. If the plant offers a firm old-crop basis on top of the futures rally, sell cash. For harvest bushels, do not lock a weak basis solely because waiver headlines are circulating.

5. Export demand is adequate, not yet strong enough to carry the rally

Status: UPDATE

What happened

Weekly sales published August 20 included 232,892 metric tons of old-crop corn and 816,097 tons of new-crop. New-crop commitments totaled 11.39 million metric tons and were roughly 30% behind the prior year. (syngenta.ca)

Why it matters for a long-corn producer

USDA raised 2026/27 exports to 3.275 billion bushels. The crop may be smaller, but the demand side still has to show up. A bullish yield story without improving export commitments leaves the market vulnerable after the September report.

What to do with it

Do not base the whole marketing plan on export acceleration that has not occurred. Use the present crop-risk premium to cover more breakeven.

The crop you still have to market

Old-crop in bins: Nearby rallied sharply. Sell more where basis is respectable. Where it is not, separate the futures and basis decisions. Old corn should not roll into harvest without a written carry calculation.

New-crop still growing: Field evidence is bullish, but your own production estimate comes first. Price an additional increment against December while preserving flexibility for the September 11 report and early yield checks.

Bottom line

The decision that changed this week is the futures sale, not automatically the basis sale. Crop Tour challenged USDA, and the board responded with roughly 30 cents. Capture part of it. Wait on weak harvest basis only if your bins, financing and expected carry can pay for that wait. The next event capable of changing the view is USDA’s September 11 yield and production update.
